Machine translation systems have no grammatical voice. They have statistics over sequences, and one of the first things those statistics quietly erase is *aspect* — how an event unfolds over time. For Spanish, the erasure shows up in the imperfecto, and the results are usually wrong in a way that is hard to see and harder to fix.

## The two pasts

Spanish has two simple past tenses, and their difference is not tense but aspect. They are both "past". What differs is the *view*:

- *Canté* (preterite): the song is over. Bounded, completed.
- *Cantaba* (imperfect): the singing was ongoing, habitual, or backgrounded. Unbounded in view.

English does not mark this on the verb. It smuggles it into periphrasis ("used to sing", "was singing") or drops it entirely. So when an English-trained model sees *cantaba*, it has to guess which of the two smuggled forms the context wants.

## Where it goes wrong

The classic failure pair:

- *Cuando llegué, ella cantaba.* → "When I arrived, she **was singing**."
- *Cuando llego, ella canta.* → "When I arrive, she sings."

The aspect is doing real work here: the first sentence pastes the arrival *into* the singing. Translate it as "she sang", and you have invented a sequence that never happened — she was already singing when the arrival happened; the sentence tells you nothing about whether she stopped.

A second, uglier failure: habitual readings get flattened into one-off events.

- *De niño, corría todos los días.* → "As a child, I **ran** every day."

"Ran" is true and loses nothing that an English speaker needs at a glance. But the model did not *understand* the iteration; it got lucky. Feed it *De niño, corría en el parque cerca de casa de mi abuela, que olía a pan* and the same luck can turn into *"As a child, I ran in the park near my grandmother's house, which smelled like bread"* — where *olía* is correct — or into nonsense when the model snaps to a preterite reading of a verb that never was one.

## What MT systems actually do

Modern NMT has no explicit aspect state, but the hidden state approximates it: the model can, in principle, hold "unbounded event" information across the clause. The problem is that *principle* becomes *practice* only when the training data makes the distinction load-bearing. English-Spanish corpus pairs are mostly aligned at the sentential level, with a strong default to the preterite because declarative narrative biographies and how-to text over-use it. Aspect is a distributional accident in the training data, not a modeled category.

## What you can do about it

For a researcher, three concrete moves:

1. **Test with aspect-heavy eval sets.** Build a mini-set of twenty minimal pairs (event-in-progress vs. completed, habitual vs. one-off) and run your system on it. A single-pass BLEU score hides the difference.
2. **Tag the training signal.** If you control the pipeline, annotate aspect on the Spanish side and add a small auxiliary loss or a prompt prefix (e.g. "traduce el siguiente texto teniendo en cuenta el aspecto"). Even noisy tags beat no tags.
3. **Accept and disclose.** If your product translates user-generated Spanish, habitual and backgrounded readings will occasionally flatten. Decide whether that is acceptable for the product, because it will happen even in good systems.

## The wider point

Aspect is one corner of the largest gap in current NLP: the difference between *mapping forms* and *modeling meaning*. The forms are finite and licitable. The meanings — iteration, boundedness, viewpoint — are grammaticalized differently in every language, which means no amount of English-centric pretraining will ever fully cover them.

The model does not know the Spanish imperfect is not imperfect. It knows sequences. Giving it the sequence-data that makes aspect load-bearing is the only fix, and it is a data problem dressed up as an architecture problem — as most translation problems ultimately are.
